Technical Mechanics

How Fuel Injectors Work

A fuel injector is an electronically controlled solenoid valve that both meters fuel flow and atomizes fuel into a combustible vapor. Positioned on the intake manifold or directly into the combustion chamber, it is sealed between the fuel rail and the engine block.

Injectors are continuously supplied with positive power when the ignition is active. The engine control computer (ECM/ECU/PCM) governs the ground side. Gathering live telemetry from oxygen, airflow and throttle sensors, the ECU triggers the ground switch, allowing electrical current to energize the internal magnetic coil.

Most injectors rely on an internal 10 to 20 micron filter basket. A minor 25% restriction in this microscopic screen forces the ECU to stretch its on-time by 25% to offset the fuel loss — driving erratic fuel trim adjustments.

The injector opens for just 1 to 20 milliseconds depending on engine demand. When the ground is removed, the electromagnetic coil de-energizes and a calibrated spring snaps the pintle needle shut against the valve seat.

Combustion Physics

The Danger of a 10% Restriction

It doesn't take total blockage to degrade an engine. A restriction of just 10% in the fuel flow of a single injector is sufficient to induce lean misfires.

When an unburned air-fuel mixture escapes into the exhaust, the upstream O2 sensor reads an apparent lean condition (excess oxygen). Because the ECU reads an aggregate exhaust stream, it automatically richens fuel delivery across all cylinders. This leads to heavy hydrocarbon soot, carbon build-up on intake valves, and catalytic converter damage.

Our 3-stage ultrasonic cleaning and back-flushing removes stubborn carbon varnish, restoring droplet atomization and returning 10% to 15% better gas mileage.
